# Genre

Genre is the form-convention contract between writer and reader. When a reader picks up a research paper, they expect an abstract, a literature review, methods, results, and discussion. When they open a how-to guide, they expect numbered steps, imperative verbs, and specific outcomes. When they read a memo, they expect brevity, clear action items, and a defined recipient.

Genre creates mutual expectation. The writer signals a genre; the reader decodes it. When a text violates genre expectations without signaling the departure, the reader experiences confusion — not because the content is wrong, but because the form is wrong.

## Genre as Layer 1 and Layer 5

Genre sits at both Layer 1 (Functional) and Layer 5 (Editorial). As a functional concern, genre selection governs the document's overall structure, purpose fulfillment, and reader relationship. As an editorial concern, genre defines the conventions — the house rules — that apply to a specific form (citation styles in academic genre, active imperatives in instructional genre, hedged claims in scientific genre).

## Genre and Audience

Genre selection is downstream of [[audience-awareness]]. Knowing your reader is a prerequisite for choosing the right form: the same information about a technical topic might be a tutorial for a novice, a reference entry for a practitioner, an explanation for an interested non-specialist, and a case study for a peer.

The Diataxis framework ([[topic-type]]) is one codification of genre at the documentation level: it defines four distinct types of technical content (tutorial, how-to, reference, explanation) and asserts that mixing them in a single piece violates reader expectations.

## Genre Violations

A genre violation occurs when the form does not match the purpose or audience:
- An instructional document written in academic style (passive, hedged, past tense) — the genre signals theory when readers need procedure
- A reference document written as narrative prose — reference documents are consulted, not read; narrative form requires sequential reading
- A persuasive document structured as neutral information — the rhetorical purpose is hidden, frustrating readers who don't know they're being persuaded

Genre violations are often invisible to the writer, who knows what kind of document they intend to write. They are highly visible to the reader, who experiences the mismatch as confusion about what to do with the text.

## Cross-Cultural Sensitivity

Genre conventions are culturally situated. What counts as appropriate formality, what level of directness is expected, how authority is established, and what a "report" looks like vary across cultures and disciplines. A reader from a different cultural or disciplinary context may not share the writer's genre assumptions.
