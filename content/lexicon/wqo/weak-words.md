# Weak Words

Weak words are vocabulary items that occupy space in a sentence without contributing specific meaning. They include:

- **Vague verbs** that narrate activity without specifying it: *utilize* (instead of *use*), *leverage* (instead of *use*, *apply*, *exploit*), *facilitate* (instead of *help*, *enable*, *allow*), *impact* as a verb (instead of *affect*, *change*, *damage*)
- **Hollow intensifiers** that add apparent emphasis while diluting meaning: *very*, *quite*, *rather*, *somewhat*, *incredibly*, *extremely* — each tells the reader that what follows is significant without saying *how*
- **Hedge words** that qualify without precision: *typically*, *often*, *generally*, *usually* — appropriate when genuine uncertainty exists, weak when used to avoid commitment
- **Throat-clearing modifiers**: *basically*, *essentially*, *fundamentally* — often signal that the claim is about to be imprecise

## Why "Utilize" Is Not a Synonym for "Use"

"Utilize" is not wrong — it has a legitimate meaning: to put something to use that was not specifically designed for that purpose. "We utilized a butter knife as a screwdriver" is correct usage. But "utilize" is pervasively used as a longer-sounding synonym for "use" — and this is the weakness. The longer word suggests precision without delivering it.

The test: if "use" conveys the same meaning, "utilize" is a weak word choice.

## Hedging vs. Appropriate Qualification

Not all hedging is weak. Epistemic hedges that reflect genuine uncertainty ("the data *suggest*..." rather than "the data *prove*...") are accurate and appropriate. Hedges become weak when they are used to avoid commitment on claims that the evidence *does* support: "this approach *often* improves outcomes" when the data show it *consistently* improves outcomes.

## Relationship to Lard Factor

[[Lard-factor]] measures the volume of unnecessary words in a sentence. Weak words contribute to lard factor (they add words without adding meaning), but the two concerns are distinct. Weak words are a vocabulary quality problem — specific words that are too vague. Lard factor is a sentence structure problem — too many words regardless of whether individual words are vague.

A sentence can have few words and still be weak: "She did things that were very good." Short but both weak and vague. A sentence can be long and contain no weak words: every word carries specific meaning even if the sentence is complex.
