# Hallucination Risk

AI language models produce confident, fluent text even when generating content that has no factual basis. In documentation contexts, this produces a specific class of failure: plausible-sounding but incorrect technical claims that are indistinguishable in tone and format from correct claims.

Hallucination risk is elevated in specific content categories: API parameter names and data types, code examples with external dependencies, citations and references, historical facts and statistics, standards references, and version-specific behavior descriptions. These are precisely the content categories most likely to be generated with AI assistance and most dangerous to get wrong.

## Detection challenges

Hallucination is difficult to detect because it does not correlate with linguistic quality. A hallucinated parameter name appears in the same sentence structure as a correct one. A fabricated citation follows the same format as a real one. The only reliable detection method is independent verification against authoritative sources — not re-reading the generated text, which tends to confirm what it says.

[[source-integrity]] is the related concern: hallucination risk is the specific failure mode that source integrity controls for in AI-assisted writing. A document with strong source integrity practices (every claim traced to a verifiable source) is resistant to hallucination, because unverified claims cannot be published.

## Writing practices that reduce risk

Confining AI assistance to tasks with verifiable outputs (code generation tested against a compiler/interpreter, paraphrasing with source text present, summarization with source document visible) substantially reduces hallucination risk. Treating AI output as a first draft that requires factual review rather than a finished product is the fundamental mitigation.

**Engineering parallel:** Hallucination risk maps to *untested code paths in production* — both involve content that appears correct in the absence of verification; both require systematic testing (unit tests / source verification) to distinguish correct from plausibly incorrect output.
