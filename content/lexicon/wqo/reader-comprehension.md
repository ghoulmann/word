# Reader Comprehension

Reader comprehension is the outcome measure that most WQO concerns ultimately serve. A text succeeds when the reader extracts the intended meaning accurately, retains it in working memory long enough to act on it, and integrates it with prior knowledge without confusion or error.

Comprehension is not a single event — it unfolds sentence by sentence, paragraph by paragraph. A reader who loses the thread at any point must either backtrack or fill the gap with inference, and inference is error-prone. Technical and professional writing, where precision matters, has especially low tolerance for comprehension gaps.

## What degrades comprehension

[[cognitive-overload]] is the most immediate threat: when working memory is saturated by structural complexity, unfamiliar vocabulary, or competing demands, the reader cannot process new information without dropping what came before.

[[coherence]] failures — logical discontinuities between claims — force readers to construct bridges the writer should have built. Each forced inference consumes cognitive resources and introduces error risk.

[[heading-clarity]] problems reduce comprehension at the navigation layer: readers who cannot accurately predict a section's content from its heading over-invest in reading it or skip it when they should not.

## The comprehension threshold

Comprehension degrades gradually before it fails completely. Moderate complexity or minor coherence problems reduce accuracy and increase reading time; severe problems cause readers to abandon the text or misinterpret key claims. For consequential documents — contracts, safety procedures, medical instructions — partial comprehension is as dangerous as none.

The [[plain-language]] movement operationalizes comprehension as a design constraint: prose is successful only when the target reader can understand it on a single reading.
