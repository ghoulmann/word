# Rhetorical Arrangement

Rhetorical arrangement is the classical term (*dispositio*) for the organizational strategy of a text. It asks: given what I need to accomplish and who I am writing for, in what order should this content appear?

Order is not neutral. The same information arranged differently achieves different effects. A persuasive document that begins with the conclusion (front-loading) reaches a different reader than one that builds to the conclusion (inductive arrangement). A tutorial that begins with prerequisites reaches a different reader than one that begins with the goal.

## Arrangement and Purpose

Arrangement is downstream of purpose. [[Hierarchy-of-purpose]] and [[audience-awareness]] establish what the document is for and who it serves; arrangement implements that purpose. A document meant to persuade a skeptical reader typically begins with common ground and builds to the conclusion. A document meant to inform a reader who knows what they're looking for typically front-loads conclusions and supports them after.

The classical arrangement (exordium, narratio, confirmatio, refutatio, peroratio) is one rhetorical template for persuasive discourse. The inverted pyramid (most important first) is a journalistic template for informative discourse. Diataxis topic types each carry their own implied arrangement. No single template suits all purposes.

## Arrangement and Coherence

[[Coherence]] is a property of the completed arrangement: once the text is organized, does the argument flow? But arrangement decisions precede coherence — you cannot evaluate whether the flow works until the organizational sequence is chosen.

Arrangement is addressed during revising, when the content is assembled and the organizational choices can be evaluated against each other. Changing arrangement during drafting is normal and expected; evaluating it too early (during first drafting) is premature.

## Common Arrangement Patterns

**Front-loaded (direct)**: conclusion first, then evidence and reasoning. Standard for business communication, journalism, executive summaries.

**Inductive (suspenseful)**: evidence and reasoning first, conclusion at the end. Standard for academic arguments, narratives, persuasive essays for skeptical readers.

**Problem-solution**: establish the problem, then propose the solution. Widely used in proposals, design documents, instructional content.

**Chronological**: events or steps in time order. Natural for tutorials, procedures, historical narratives.

**Spatial or topical**: organized by location or category rather than by argument or time. Used for reference content, catalogs, comparative analyses.

**Engineering parallel:** Rhetorical arrangement maps to *solution sequencing in design review* — both require that criteria precede evaluation and problem precede solution; both fail when conclusions appear before the evidence needed to judge them.
