# Rhetorical Situation

The rhetorical situation is the context that makes a piece of writing necessary and that constrains how it must be written. Lloyd Bitzer, in his 1968 essay "The Rhetorical Situation," identified three constitutive elements: **exigence** (the imperfection or urgency that calls writing into being), **audience** (those who can be influenced and who can act on the text), and **constraints** (the persons, events, objects, and relations that limit what can be said and how). Together these three determine not only what a text should accomplish but what form it must take to accomplish it.

No compositional choice is neutral with respect to the rhetorical situation. Word choice, level of formality, document length, organizational strategy, use of evidence — all of these are answers to questions that the rhetorical situation poses. A writer who begins drafting without analyzing the situation answers these questions implicitly and unreliably. A writer who analyzes the situation before drafting answers them intentionally.

## The Three Components

**Exigence** is the reason writing is called for — not the assigned topic, but the underlying need the text must address. A product recall notice has a clear exigence: a safety risk that readers must know about and act on. A quarterly report has an exigence: decision-makers need information to allocate resources. Identifying exigence prevents the commonest failure of purpose: writing that describes a situation without addressing the urgency that made description necessary.

**Audience** in the rhetorical sense is not a demographic but a functional category: those who can be moved by the text and who have the capacity to act on it. [[Audience-awareness]] asks who they are; rhetorical situation analysis asks what they can do with this text, what they already know and believe, and what they need to know and believe in order to act. The same information presented to two different audiences in two different situations may require two entirely different texts.

**Constraints** include everything that limits the range of possible responses: the author's credibility and relationship to the subject (see [[ethos-pathos-logos]]), the conventions of the genre, organizational or legal requirements, the audience's prior beliefs and expectations, and the medium. Constraints are not obstacles — they are the materials the writer works with. Recognizing them prevents false choices ("I can't write it that way") and hidden assumptions ("of course it should be written that way").

## Why It Is Pre-Compositional

Rhetorical situation analysis must precede composition because its outputs are the inputs to every downstream decision. [[Hierarchy-of-purpose]] cannot be established without knowing the exigence. [[Audience-awareness]] cannot be calibrated without knowing who the audience is and what they need to do. [[Genre]] cannot be chosen without knowing what form the situation calls for. [[Rhetorical-arrangement]] cannot be planned without knowing what the audience already believes and what sequence of information will move them toward the text's purpose.

This is why the triage principle — address functional concerns before structural, structural before mechanical — has the rhetorical situation at its root. A document that has been edited to mechanical perfection but that misidentifies its audience or misunderstands its exigence is perfectly wrong. No amount of line editing rescues a text that answers the wrong question.

## Rhetorical Situation and the Triangle

The classical rhetorical triangle — author (ethos), audience (pathos), subject/logos — maps onto Bitzer's framework but from a different angle. The triangle describes the relationships among three elements of any communication act. The rhetorical situation describes the context that activates those relationships. Analyzing the situation tells the writer how to position each vertex: how much authority to claim, what the audience's values and expectations are, and what the subject demands by way of evidence and argument.

In practice, [[ethos-pathos-logos]] analysis happens as part of rhetorical situation analysis, not separate from it.

## Failure Modes

A text that fails to analyze its rhetorical situation typically produces one of these defects:

- **Wrong genre** — the situation called for a procedure, the writer produced an explanation.
- **Wrong audience calibration** — the text assumes expertise the audience doesn't have, or condescends to an expert.
- **Wrong level of urgency** — the text is appropriately informative but fails to motivate action in a situation that required action.
- **Wrong constraints** — the text violates genre conventions, organizational requirements, or audience expectations in ways that undermine credibility.

**Engineering parallel:** Rhetorical situation maps to the *problem statement* — both define the context that constrains all downstream choices; both fail when the constraints are wrong or unexamined, producing a solution perfectly fitted to the wrong problem.
