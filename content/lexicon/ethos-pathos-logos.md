# Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Aristotle identified three modes through which a speaker or writer persuades an audience. These are not optional extras in persuasive writing — they are the dimensions of the persuasive task. A text that relies only on logos (argument) while ignoring ethos (credibility) and pathos (emotional resonance) will persuade only readers who are already disposed to be convinced by argument alone. Most readers are not.

## The Three Appeals

**Ethos** — the appeal to the author's character, credibility, and trustworthiness. The reader's first question is: "Why should I trust this person?" Ethos is established through demonstrated expertise, appropriate credentials, transparency about limitations, consistent alignment between stated values and actions, and the quality of the writing itself (a writer who makes grammatical errors and logical fallacies undermines ethos).

**Pathos** — the appeal to the reader's emotions, values, and imagination. Pathos is not manipulation; it is engagement with what the reader actually cares about. Relevant evidence, concrete examples, narrative, and appeals to shared values are all legitimate pathos. Pathos becomes illegitimate when it displaces argument: emotional manipulation that bypasses logical evaluation is an [[informal-fallacy]] (specifically, an appeal to emotion).

**Logos** — the appeal to reason: the logical structure of the argument, the quality of the evidence, the validity of the inferences. A text built on logos alone risks being technically correct but unconvincing because it does not engage the reader's values (pathos) or establish the author's trustworthiness (ethos).

## Balance and Audience

The appropriate balance of the three appeals depends on audience and purpose. A scientific paper relies heavily on logos, establishes ethos through methodology and citation, and uses pathos sparingly (the significance statement that opens the paper). A charity fundraising letter relies heavily on pathos, establishes ethos through organizational credibility, and uses logos to show that donations achieve results.

Misreading the audience's expectation — deploying heavy pathos in a technical context, or relying on logos-only argumentation for an audience that requires emotional engagement — is a functional failure in [[audience-awareness]].

## Fallacies as Appeal Failures

The [[informal-fallacy]] taxonomy maps onto appeal misuse:
- **Ethos failures**: ad hominem attacks (impugning ethos to dismiss argument), false authority (invoking irrelevant credentials)
- **Pathos failures**: appeal to emotion without argument, appeal to fear as a substitute for evidence
- **Logos failures**: non-sequitur, circular reasoning, hasty generalization, false dichotomy
