# Tone, Voice, and Cadence

Three related but distinct concepts govern the experiential quality of prose — how it sounds and feels to a reader beyond what it says:

**Tone** is the affective register: the emotional attitude the text projects toward the reader, the subject, and the situation. Formal or informal. Warm or distant. Confident or hedged. Urgent or calm. Tone is relational — it positions the writer in relation to the reader.

**Voice** is the consistent stylistic personality of the text: the recognizable "who" behind the sentences. Voice emerges from the cumulative pattern of word choices, sentence structures, and rhetorical moves. An organization with a strong house voice sounds like itself across documents; an author with a developed voice sounds like themselves across texts.

**Cadence** is the rhythmic pattern of prose: the variation in sentence length, the balance of long and short sentences, the placement of stressed syllables, the use of parallelism. Prose with good cadence has a rhythm that carries the reader forward; prose with poor cadence feels choppy, monotonous, or effortful.

## Tone as an Editorial Concern

Tone is Editorial (Layer 5) because it is context-dependent and convention-governed, not universally correct. The right tone is the tone appropriate for:
- The reader relationship (peer, supervisor, customer, public)
- The genre (academic, instructional, journalistic, creative)
- The topic (serious, celebratory, cautionary)
- The organization's house style

Tone mismatches produce [[reader-trust]] failures: overly casual tone in a formal context signals carelessness; overly formal tone in a friendly context signals distance or condescension.

## Voice Consistency

A consistent voice is an editorial quality marker. Inconsistent voice — where the prose shifts register mid-document — signals revision by multiple authors without coordination, or a writer who hasn't found their register for this piece. Inconsistency is jarring because it interrupts the reader's relationship with the text.

Voice is governed by [[house-style]] in organizational contexts: brand voice guidelines specify vocabulary, formality level, use of first person, and the register of address to the reader.

## Cadence in Practice

Cadence is the most intuitive and least explicitly taught of the three. A prose passage with cadence varies sentence length deliberately: short sentences for emphasis, longer sentences for context and nuance, with a pattern that doesn't become predictable. The worst cadence errors are monotony (every sentence the same length) and the long-sentence wall (every sentence complex, no relief for the reader).

Reading prose aloud is the most reliable cadence diagnostic — ear catches what eye misses.

## Cross-Cultural Sensitivity

Tone conventions are culturally situated. Directness that reads as confident in one cultural context reads as rude or aggressive in another. Hedging that reads as appropriate academic caution in one context reads as evasive or uncertain in another. For cross-cultural readers, tone calibration requires explicit awareness of the target cultural register, not just the organizational style guide.
